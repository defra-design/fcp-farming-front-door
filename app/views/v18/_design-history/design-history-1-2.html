Design history Land summary (v1.2)

Third iteration of a prototype that re-presents the existing land summary customer journey using the Defra interactive mapping component (opens in new tab) (v0.0.22-alpha).

This iteration focused on the land parcel page. We:

  • made land covers on the map clickable
  • simplified the Key and Layers panels
  • applied both changes to the 2 historical land parcel views (SX3963 2340 and SX3964 7821)
  • added side by side and stacked pages to compare a previous version of a parcel with the current one
  • added 4 variants that show both versions on a single map

User research on v1.1

Round 10 of user research tested v1.1 in moderated remote usability sessions. Participants used their own or representative land parcels and thought aloud.

There were 7 participants: farmers, land managers, estate managers, environmental advisers, agents and experienced GIS users. Holdings ranged from small holdings and commercial farms to environmental organisations and large estates. One participant manages about 930 parcels.

Users could find and review parcels and valued historic mapping, but many were not confident they had found every change. The main challenge was confidence, not navigation.

What we learned about users

Users are trying to verify information, not simply view it

Users mainly used mapping to check that the RPA's understanding of their land matched their own records. Participants described:

  • checking boundary updates
  • reviewing land cover classifications
  • challenging changes made by the RPA
  • validating information before applications or claims
  • comparing mapping against local knowledge or other systems

"Sometimes it comes through and we think that isn't right." (P1)

"RPA are constantly tinkering with boundaries and changing shapes." (P11)

What this means for the design

Users need tools that support validation and verification, not simply viewing.

Confidence matters more than task completion

The biggest usability challenge was confidence, not task completion. Most participants completed the tasks, but many were unsure whether they had:

  • found all the relevant information
  • identified all the changes
  • correctly interpreted the mapping information
  • reached the correct conclusion

"No, not at all." (P8, when asked how confident they were they had found all the changes)

"How do I know how it's changed?" (P2)

What this means for the design

Future improvements should help users feel confident in their understanding, not simply let them complete tasks.

Historic mapping solves a genuine user problem

Historic mapping was one of the most valued parts of the prototype. Participants understood it immediately and named real uses, including:

  • auditing changes
  • validating mapping
  • challenging updates
  • investigating parcel history
  • supporting environmental applications

"That functionality was great." (P8)

"I think that's a very good thing." (P4)

What this means for the design

Historic mapping should be treated as a validated opportunity.

Users expect mapping tools to behave like mapping tools

Many usability issues came from missing behaviours that participants expected from tools they already use: RPS mapping, GIS software such as ArcGIS and Cadcorp, Gatekeeper, Land App and other professional mapping tools. They expected:

  • click to highlight
  • hover to highlight
  • measurement tools
  • layer controls
  • transparency controls
  • data linked to the map
What this means for the design

The prototype should align more closely with established mapping interaction patterns where possible.

Adviser and agent workflows differ from farmer workflows

Advisers, estate managers and environmental professionals often had different needs from farmers. They were more likely to talk about:

  • multiple parcels
  • exports
  • grouping parcels
  • bulk workflows
  • auditing history
  • GIS integration
What this means for the design

Future design work should consider whether different user groups need different workflows or capabilities.

What this means for the design overall

The research suggests the design should:

  • support two-way interactions between information panels and mapped features
  • help users understand changes, not simply view them
  • support validation using satellite imagery
  • improve confidence rather than simply task completion
  • support complex workflows involving multiple parcels
  • better explain land cover information and classifications
  • accommodate both parcel ID and field name based workflows

Recommended actions

Immediate priorities

  1. Strengthen the interactions between the map and the information panel.
  2. Improve confidence identifying changes.
  3. Make historic mapping easier to find.
  4. Improve how mapping changes are explained.

Next iteration

  1. Test side by side comparison concepts.
  2. Explore transparency controls for imagery.
  3. Make land cover information easier to interpret.
  4. Review layer terminology and descriptions.

Longer-term opportunities

  1. Support multi-parcel workflows.
  2. Explore parcel naming.
  3. Show scheme information directly against parcels.
  4. Improve accessibility support.

Areas for further research

  • multi-parcel workflows
  • large estates and holdings
  • measurement functionality
  • parcel names and identifiers
  • Countryside Stewardship and SFI integration
  • accessibility requirements
  • bulk parcel management
  • filtering and grouping concepts
  • visualising historic changes

Selecting a land cover on the map

Land covers are now clickable, as hedgerows were in v1.1. This responds to the strongest usability finding from the research: 6 of 7 participants expected the map and information panel to be linked.

When a user clicks a land cover:

  • its row in the Land covers table is highlighted and made bold
  • it is outlined on the map
  • clicking anywhere else on the map clears the selection

Where a hedgerow and a land cover overlap, the hedgerow takes the click, so only one thing is highlighted. On mobile, the fullscreen map's touch crosshair can also select a land cover.

Technical notes

The interact plugin misses clicks on polygons that cross a vector tile boundary, which is common when zoomed in to one parcel. We used the map library's own hit test instead, and the prototype draws the selection outline itself.

Key panel

We hid the "Land cover" group heading in the Key. The Key only shows land covers and hedgerows, so the heading added noise. It is visually hidden, not removed, so screen reader users keep the grouping.

Layers panel

Each land cover type can now be switched on and off individually, so a user can isolate one classification on the map. This responds to the research: participants expected GIS-style layer controls, and 4 of 7 struggled to match land cover classifications to areas on the map.

In v1.1 the panel had 3 toggles: Land covers, Land parcel outlines and Hedgerows. Now:

  • each land cover type on the parcel has its own checkbox, indented under a "Land cover" parent checkbox
  • the parent switches all types together, and stays ticked while any type is ticked
  • only the types on the current parcel are listed
  • the land parcel outline toggles are removed, as the outline is the context for everything else

Choices survive a change of base map, and reset when the user moves to a different parcel.

Technical notes

The mapping component has no parent checkbox for a group of layers, so the prototype adds one. This is a prototype workaround and would need to be raised with the interactive map team for the real service.

Comparing land parcel versions

We added a page that shows a previous and the current version of a parcel on one screen. In v1.1 users had to move between the 2 versions and remember the differences. In the research, 4 of 7 participants asked for an easier way to compare, such as side by side views.

The page is linked from a "Compare versions" row in the land parcel summary and a "Compare with current version" link on the historical view. Both only appear for parcels with a previous version (SX3963 2340 and SX3964 7821). The row also signposts historic mapping from the current parcel page, which 2 of 7 participants could not find without help.

Layout

Each version has its own column with a map and, below it, the Summary, Land covers and Hedgerows tables from the land parcel page. A highlighted panel gives the previous version's date range, and each column repeats its date so the tables line up. On small screens the columns stack.

Unlike the land parcel page, this page scrolls normally, with the maps in fixed-height frames above the tables.

Linked maps

Panning or zooming one map moves the other, so both show the same area. Both open framed on the parcel. Each map keeps its own Key, Layers panel and base map, so a user can, for example, view the current version on aerial imagery.

Clicking a land cover or hedgerow highlights its row in that column's tables, as on the land parcel page.

Marking the differences

The tables mark each difference, so users do not have to spot every change themselves. In the research, 5 of 7 participants were not confident they had found all the changes, and 5 of 7 wanted to understand what had changed.

A tag in the first cell of a row shows:

  • Added on a current row with no equivalent in the previous version
  • Removed on a previous row with no equivalent in the current version
  • Changed on both rows where a land cover or hedgerow has a different area or length

A changed value on the current side also has an up or down arrow. It appears only on the current side, as an arrow beside the previous value would suggest that value had moved. Screen reader users hear "increased" or "decreased" and the previous value. Summary rows are marked the same way when totals differ.

Technical notes

Land covers are matched by name, and covers of the same type are paired in area order. Hedgerows have no stable identity between versions, so they are matched by position. This works for the 2 example parcels but would need real identifiers in the live service.

The current version is read live from the land covers service, which only returns covers valid today. The previous version comes from a snapshot file stored with the prototype. The parcel boundary and hedgerows are fetched once and shown on both sides, as only the land covers differ in the examples.

The mapping component is not designed for 2 maps on a page: its plugins cache setup from the first map and reuse it for the second. The prototype gives each map its own copy of each plugin and its own layer identifiers. This is a prototype workaround and would need to be raised with the interactive map team if the real service needs 2 maps on a page.

Comparing versions on a single map

The product owner reviewed the two-map pages and asked to see both versions on one map, each switchable on and off. We built 4 variants to test different ways of switching. They share one map and one set of data, and differ in the control used.

How the two versions are drawn together

Both versions use the same colours, so the previous version is drawn underneath the current one, faded to about a third of its strength, with a dashed dark outline round each cover. The outline shows where a previous boundary sat even under a current cover. A removed cover keeps its colour so its type can still be read. The Key has a single "Previous version" entry rather than listing every type twice.

The parcel boundary and hedgerows are the same in both versions, so they are drawn once and the version controls do not affect them.

One table instead of two columns

The layers panel and changes outlined pages have one set of tables under the map. The rows describe the current version, with the same tags and arrows as the two-map pages. Removed covers are listed at the end of the Land covers table with their previous area and a Removed tag, and screen reader users hear "previous version" after that area. Summary counts and table totals are marked the same way.

Clicking a cover on the map highlights one row:

  • a current cover highlights its own row
  • a previous cover highlights the matching current row, and the map outlines both shapes
  • a cover that no longer exists highlights its Removed row

Clicking a row does the same in reverse.
